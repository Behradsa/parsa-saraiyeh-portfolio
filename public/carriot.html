CASE STUDY

Carriot.

A cloud-based last-mile delivery and dispatch platform — I rebuilt its brand and redesigned the platform its dispatchers run the fleet from.

Carriot
WHEN Feb 2022 — Feb 2023 · Tehran
ROLE Graphic Designer · UI Designer
TEAMA three-person product team: a PM, a front-end developer, and me on design.
SCOPE Brand book, logotype, illustration, fleet platform UI — 50+ screens, 30+ component UI kit
LIVE carriot.ir
THE PROBLEM

Every new customer brought a requirement the last one never had — and all of them had to live in one panel.

Carriot was winning customers quickly, and the company’s strategy was to meet each fleet’s own logistics needs rather than sell one fixed workflow. A pharmaceutical distributor needed the cabin temperature recorded on the vehicle itself; a snack and beverage distributor needed to know the moment a driver left the delivery zone.

Each request arrived as one customer’s feature rather than a pattern, so the panel was growing by accretion. And the logistics team was attached to the manual routing it already trusted — so breadth and adoption were not two problems but one.

Key decisions.

1

Rebuilt the identity from the mark out.

The old lettering fell apart at small sizes and could not survive an app icon or a single-colour print, so I rebuilt it on even weights and spacing. The illustration moved to 3D for the same reason: flat drawings were not carrying what the product actually moves.

THE COST every 3D asset costs more production time than a flat one.
2

Made the panel configurable per customer, not per release.

Pharma fleets, snack routes and beverage distributors each needed something the others did not. Instead of one panel holding every control, the product became a core set of screens plus modules — cabin temperature, safe area, driver scoring — switched on per account.

THE COST every module is one more thing sales has to scope and support has to explain.
3

Turned cargo theft into a design object: the safe area.

One customer kept losing cargo to drivers selling it en route. We made the safe area a first-class thing in the product — a zone set per vehicle, and a warning the moment it is left — so the alert arrives with a reason attached, not just a position.

THE COST it puts drivers under watch, so the zone is scoped per vehicle rather than following the person all day.
4

Designed smart routing to advise the dispatcher, not replace them.

The hardest part was not the interface. The logistics team trusted its own routing, had interests tied to it, and read a smart system as a threat to the role. So the suggestion shows its reasoning and the dispatcher keeps the final assignment — and that is why they took it up.

THE COST keeping a human in the loop caps how far the routing can optimise.

Sign in — where the new brand lands first.
The identity where it ends up: on the vehicle.
OUTCOME

The team that resisted smart routing ended up running on it.

Running costs came down and vehicles were allocated and spread better across the delivery area. The brand book and the website went live, and the platform work — 50+ screens and a 30+ component UI kit — became the reference the team built against, with modules that let one product serve fleets from pharmaceutical cold chain to snacks and beverages.
